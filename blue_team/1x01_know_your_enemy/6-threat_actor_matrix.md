# MedDefense Health Systems — Threat Actor Matrix

## Purpose

This matrix consolidates the threat-actor analysis completed in Project `1x01_know_your_enemy` and maps each actor type to MedDefense's internal posture from Project `1x00_first_watch`.

The assessment distinguishes **likelihood** from **capability**. A highly capable actor is not automatically a high-priority threat if MedDefense is unlikely to attract that actor, while a lower-sophistication actor may still represent a serious risk when MedDefense's exposure makes successful compromise likely.

# Top 3 Priority Ranking

## 1. Ransomware Groups / Organized Crime — **Highest Priority**

Ransomware represents the strongest combination of **high likelihood and Critical potential impact**. MedDefense is not merely part of a generally targeted sector: its 350-bed regional-hospital profile closely matches the victim type described in Task 0, it holds valuable Restricted patient information, it depends on systems whose outage can affect clinical care, and nearby hospitals have recently suffered serious ransomware incidents. The internal posture also maps almost step-for-step to a RaaS attack lifecycle: weak MFA can support initial credential access (**GAP-007**), fragmented monitoring can allow reconnaissance and privilege escalation (**GAP-011**), the flat Central network enables lateral movement (**GAP-001**), and locally concentrated backup infrastructure increases the chance that recovery is disrupted (**GAP-008**). A successful event could therefore combine patient-data theft, EHR disruption, financial loss and prolonged clinical interruption.

## 2. Insider — Negligent — **Second Priority**

Negligent insiders rank second because the **likelihood is high and the exposure is continuous**. Unlike an external attacker, an employee does not need to defeat the perimeter: staff already possess legitimate access to systems and data required for patient care. MedDefense has repeatedly observed the conditions that make this threat credible, including shared PACS credentials, an unattended authenticated EHR session, an unmanaged personal laptop and Dr. Patel's personal NAS. **GAP-012** and **GAP-013** show that devices can fall outside formal governance, while **GAP-007** and **GAP-011** mean identity use is not consistently strongly controlled or continuously analysed. One negligent action may cause direct disclosure, but it can also create the initial foothold that an external attacker later exploits. This makes negligent insider risk both a direct privacy threat and an amplifier of ransomware and opportunistic attacks.

## 3. Unskilled / Opportunistic Attacker — **Third Priority**

Opportunistic attackers rank third because MedDefense has **high exposure even though these actors may not deliberately target the organisation by name**. Automated scanners and public exploits make any Internet-facing weakness discoverable at scale, and MedDefense already has evidence of a crypto-mining compromise consistent with opportunistic exploitation. The principal concern is not the actor's technical sophistication but the environment they encounter after finding a foothold: **GAP-016** can leave known vulnerabilities exploitable, **GAP-011** can allow commodity malicious activity to remain unnoticed, and **GAP-001** can turn a compromise of a lower-value system into a route toward Critical assets. Their potential impact is generally less deliberate and coordinated than organized ransomware, but MedDefense's architecture means even a low-skill compromise can escalate into a significant clinical or data-security event.

---

# Overall Board-Level Judgment

MedDefense should worry most about actors whose **common attack methods align directly with weaknesses already documented internally**. Ransomware groups are the primary external threat because they actively target healthcare and can convert MedDefense's segmentation, monitoring, identity and recovery gaps into organization-wide disruption. Negligent insiders are the most persistent internal threat because normal healthcare access creates daily opportunities for unsafe behavior. Opportunistic attackers remain a major exposure because they need no special interest in MedDefense: a single known vulnerability or weak credential may be sufficient to start an intrusion.

Nation-state APTs have the highest technical capability but currently low targeting likelihood; hacktivists have plausible public-facing targets but little current motivation to select MedDefense; malicious insiders can cause severe damage but are less frequent than negligent insider events. The priority is therefore not determined by sophistication alone, but by the combination of **likelihood, access opportunity and potential impact on MedDefense's Critical clinical services**.
