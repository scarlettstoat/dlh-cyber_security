## Part 2 - What Happened?

Alice and Bob each created a private key that stayed on their own computer and a public key that could safely be exchanged over the network. Alice combined her private key with Bob's public key, while Bob combined his private key with Alice's public key. Diffie-Hellman mathematics causes both calculations to produce the same shared secret even though the two sides started with different private keys. An eavesdropper such as Eve would be able to see the public parameters and the two public keys travelling across the network. However, she would not see either private key, and deriving the shared secret from the public information alone is designed to be computationally infeasible. Alice and Bob can therefore establish a common secret without transmitting that secret directly.

## Part 3 - Man-in-the-Middle Attack

Plain Diffie-Hellman does not prove who a public key belongs to, so an active attacker can replace Alice's and Bob's public keys while they are being exchanged. Eve could perform one DH exchange with Alice and a separate DH exchange with Bob, giving her one shared secret with each side while Alice and Bob incorrectly believe they are communicating directly. If the MedDefense VPN tunnel between Central and Westside used unauthenticated DH, an attacker on the network path could potentially decrypt traffic from one side, inspect or modify it, re-encrypt it and forward it to the other side. Certificates prevent this by binding a public key to an authenticated identity and allowing each endpoint to verify that the key really belongs to the expected system. If Eve substituted her own key, certificate or handshake authentication would fail unless she possessed a trusted credential for the identity she was impersonating.
